01 — Context

The Business Behind the Camera

Our SaaS product enables consumer goods companies to verify that retailers are maintaining cooler and shelf layouts per the agreed planogram — the contractual arrangement of products mandated by brands. Sales reps visit hundreds of outlets each week, capturing image sequences of coolers and shelves as evidence of compliance. These images feed into backend quality review workflows, Machine Learning models, and client-facing compliance dashboards.

The integrity of the entire compliance program rested on image quality. A blurry shot, a closed cooler door, or a photo taken from a bad angle produced useless data — and in some cases, actively misleading compliance scores. When a rep submitted a "Picture of Picture" (photographing a previous visit's printout instead of the actual shelf), it was an act of fraud that cost brands real money.

⚠️ Scale context: The field force averaged 600+ reps across active markets, each visiting 12–18 outlets per day. At that scale, a 15% bad-image rate translated to thousands of rejections per week — each requiring human review, re-visit scheduling, and client communication overhead.

02 — Problem Definition

A Broken Quality Loop

When the back-office QC team or the backend ML pipeline rejected an image, the sequence was already hours — sometimes days — old. The rep had left the store, moved on to other outlets, and often had no memory of what the cooler looked like. The rejection meant one of three things: the visit was marked invalid, the rep was asked to return, or the data gap was silently absorbed as missing compliance evidence. None of those outcomes were good.

Pain 01

Delayed rejection loop. Backend QC caught quality issues hours or days after capture. Reps had already left the outlet, making retakes logistically impossible without a costly second visit.

Pain 02

Manual review overhead. A dedicated QC team spent significant hours daily triaging rejected images — tagging error types, routing for re-visit, and updating client reports. This was entirely reactive and unscalable.

Pain 03

Fraudulent submissions eroding trust. "Picture of Picture" and other intentional quality violations were indistinguishable from accidental errors at the point of capture. Clients were paying for compliance data they couldn't trust.

Pain 04

Lost compliance revenue. When valid data couldn't be produced for an outlet visit, brands lost the ability to claim planogram compliance bonuses — a contractually significant sum per outlet per audit cycle.

Pain 05

Rep confusion about standards. Reps had no real-time feedback on what "good" looked like. Many errors (wrong angle, incomplete scene, missing overlap) were honest mistakes that could have been corrected on-the-spot with timely guidance.

Pain 06

Client-visible quality gaps. Compliance dashboards showed data holes attributable to image quality failures — a direct signal to enterprise clients that the data collection process was unreliable.

🔴 The core insight: The rejection happened at the wrong end of the process. Moving quality enforcement from back-office review to the point of capture — in the rep's hands, while they were still standing in front of the cooler — was the only way to make retakes possible without added cost.

04 — Problem Framing

Not All Errors Are Equal

A critical design decision early in the project: the system needed to treat different error types differently. Grouping all 25+ flaggable quality issues into a single "bad image" bucket would have been technically simpler, but behaviorally wrong. Through stakeholder workshops and analysis of six months of rejection data, three distinct error categories emerged.

Operator Error
Correctable in the Moment

Bad angle, not enough overlap, closed door, wrong scene type, image blur, incomplete scene. These are honest mistakes a rep can fix immediately by retaking the photo. The system should encourage a retake, not penalise.

Intentional Violation
Fraud Signal

Picture of Picture (PoP). This is a rep photographing a prior visit's printout as if it were a live shelf. It cannot be an accident. Detecting this at capture closes the exploit before submission and creates an audit trail.

System / Environment
Contextual — Inform, Don't Block

Too dark, too bright, duplicate images, empty coolers/shelves, body part in frame, junk image. Some of these are legitimate conditions the rep can't control. The system should surface them for awareness without hard-blocking submission.

Error Source Breakdown — Based on 6 Months of Rejection Data

What the Back-Office Was Seeing
✕ ~38% of rejections were Operator Errors — correctable if the rep had known at capture time. Zero retake opportunity existed in the current flow.
✕ "Picture of Picture" violations accounted for a disproportionate share of fraud-related compliance disputes with enterprise clients — yet detecting them required manual pixel-level inspection.
✕ The "Is Usable?" binary at the end of the QC form (see desktop tool screenshot) was the only output of an hours-long review process — a one-bit answer to a nuanced problem.
✕ Multiple error types could co-exist on a single image (e.g. "Closed Door" + "Bad Angle" + "Too Dark"), but QC tooling did not surface all flags together in an actionable way.
What the System Needed to Communicate
✓ Surface all applicable error flags simultaneously, not sequentially, so the rep sees the full picture in a single glance.
✓ Distinguish between "you can fix this right now" and "this may be an environment issue" — different calls to action for different error types.
✓ Make the Ignore / Retake decision require intentional effort — not a passive dismiss. The rep should consciously choose to submit a flagged image.
✓ Log all detections and rep decisions server-side — creating an audit trail that supports fraud investigation and model training simultaneously.

05 — Architecture & Design

Shifting Quality Left — From Backend to Capture

The system was architected as a layered quality gate running immediately after the Android camera shutter. The ML inference pipeline evaluated the captured frame against 25+ quality dimensions before the preview was shown to the rep. This introduced a sub-2-second feedback window — fast enough to feel instantaneous, meaningful enough to allow a retake before the rep moved their device.

💡 Design implication from the QC tool analysis: The back-office taxonomy was comprehensive and well-categorised — 25 named error types across visual quality, scene composition, and content validity dimensions. We didn't invent a new language; we moved an existing expert vocabulary to the point of capture.

06 — Design Decisions

What the Feedback System Had to Get Right

The mobile error sheet (shown in the second screenshot) is the product of several deliberate design decisions. Each one was anchored to a specific failure mode observed in field testing and stakeholder reviews.

⚠️
Show All Errors at Once vs. One-at-a-Time Progressive Disclosure Completeness vs. Cognitive load ▾

An early prototype surfaced errors one at a time — "fix this, then see the next issue." The intent was to reduce overwhelm. In field testing, reps hated it. They retook the image after fixing one issue, only to be told there was another. It felt like a hidden-ball trick, and several reps abandoned the retake flow entirely after two rounds.

Decision: All detected errors are surfaced simultaneously in a single scrollable sheet, displayed as a list of warning items with consistent triangular caution icons. Reps can see the full picture at once — eight flags is a lot, but it's honest. The sheet header ("The image you captured has the following errors:") frames it neutrally, not as an accusation.

This also gave back-office context: when a rep chose "Ignore Error" on a multi-flag image, the full list was logged. Reviewers could see what was knowingly bypassed, enabling smarter audit targeting.

✓ Full error list shown · Simultaneous display · Scroll for overflow · All flags logged
🔁
Two-Button Decision — "Ignore Error" vs. "Retake Image" Rep autonomy vs. Error elimination ▾

A hard block — preventing submission when errors were detected — was considered and rejected for several reasons. Some error flags represent genuine field conditions the rep cannot control: a legitimately empty cooler triggers "Empty Coolers/Shelves," and a mandatory retake would penalise the rep for accurate documentation. Absolute blocks also breed workarounds.

Decision: Both options are presented with equal visual weight — both rendered in the same red/coral colour with no visual hierarchy between them. This was intentional. We wanted the rep to read both options before acting, not default-tap the rightmost button. The choice requires conscious selection, and the decision is recorded either way.

For the specific case of "Picture of Picture" — a fraud signal — the system flags it identically to other errors in the UI, but the server-side logic routes it to a dedicated fraud audit queue regardless of which button the rep taps. The UI stays neutral; the backend consequence is not.

✓ No hard block · Equal-weight buttons · Conscious choice required · Fraud flagged server-side regardless
🖼️
Show Captured Image Behind the Error Sheet vs. Clean Modal Context while deciding vs. Distraction-free focus ▾

An early version used a full-screen modal with a neutral grey background. Reps in testing frequently asked "which image is this for?" — especially during sequences where multiple images were captured before the error sheet appeared. Without seeing the image, the error list felt abstract.

Decision: The error sheet is displayed as a partial-screen bottom sheet overlaying the captured image. The rep can see what image triggered the flags — "Captured from Bad Angle" is immediately interpretable when you can see the photo it refers to. The overlay preserves spatial context without requiring the rep to navigate away to check.

✓ Image visible behind sheet · Bottom-sheet overlay pattern · Context preserved
🧭
On-Device Inference vs. Server-Round-Trip for Detection Latency vs. Model accuracy ceiling ▾

A server-side detection approach would have allowed heavier models and easier updates, but introduced a critical dependency: network connectivity. Field reps often operate in basement retail environments, refrigeration sections with poor signal, and rural markets with unreliable data. A detection system that silently failed when offline would have been worse than no system at all.

Decision: Core quality detectors — angle, blur, overlap, scene completeness, brightness — run on-device using a quantised lightweight model deployable via the standard app update mechanism. Server-side models handle the heavier classification tasks (scene type validation, PoP detection) asynchronously on upload, with results feeding the back-office audit queue rather than blocking the rep. The on-device gate addresses the correctables; the server gate addresses the fraud signals.

✓ On-device for correctables · Server-side for fraud/scene · Offline-resilient architecture
📋
Unified Error Language Bridging Mobile UI and QC Back-Office Consistency vs. Simplification for mobile ▾

When a rep tapped "Ignore Error" and the image went through to back-office review, the reviewer saw the same error flag names the rep had been shown. This was a deliberate consistency choice. In early builds, we used simplified rep-facing labels ("Photo is Blurry") and technical QC labels ("Image Blur") — and the disconnect caused confusion in escalation conversations.

Decision: The mobile error labels match the QC tool taxonomy exactly: "Captured from Bad Angle," "Not Enough Overlap," "Multiple Disconnected POS in one Scene." Reps learning the terms on mobile become fluent in the same vocabulary the backend team uses — reducing friction in supervisor coaching, rep feedback, and client audit discussions. Terminology alignment was a system design choice, not just a content choice.

✓ Same error labels mobile ↔ QC tool · Shared vocabulary · Audit trail coherence

10 — Reflection

What This Project Taught Me About System Thinking

The surface deliverable was a mobile error sheet — a bottom sheet with a list of warning icons. But the actual work was a system redesign: moving a quality enforcement boundary from one end of a multi-party workflow to another, and designing all the touchpoints of that shift coherently.

The back-office QC tool, the mobile error UX, the ML model's classification taxonomy, the server-side audit logging schema, the rep training materials — all of these had to speak the same language and operate as a single coherent system. The moment any layer used different terminology or different categorisation logic, the audit trail broke and the data became incomparable.

The most impactful design decision in the whole project was not a UI component. It was the decision to use the existing QC taxonomy as the shared ontology across all layers. That single alignment choice made the system debuggable, trainable, and auditable in ways that no amount of interface polish could have compensated for.
